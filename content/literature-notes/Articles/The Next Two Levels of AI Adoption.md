## Highlights
- In June, we published the eight levels of AI adoption, giving people a map of where they are with AI today and what it takes to get to the next level. The map ran from Level 1, where you ask a chatbot a question, to Level 8, where a manager agent runs a team of subagents for you. I expected most readers to find themselves in the bottom half, and most of them did. What I didn’t expect was how quickly the people at the top would reach the end of the map. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01m7fbgg18es2n21qzbpqpsb7p))
- Today, most engineers I talk to are running several long-running agents at once and handing the coordination work to an orchestrator. Even nontechnical teams are progressing faster than I thought they would, because the Codex and Claude desktop apps make orchestration much easier for knowledge work. As more people approach the top end of the eight levels we outlined in June, I keep getting asked, “What comes next?” My answer, which I covered in my talk at Thesis: 2027 last week, is simulation: building a model of the world your agents work in, so you can test strategies before reality does. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01m7fbgk362jqs9ze8bdqqvrs4))
- In the original framework, each level let you hand off longer stretches of work and gave the AI more autonomy, with orchestration representing the most you could hand off. Today, there’s no reason to believe that pattern should end with orchestration. Once you have agents managing and executing the work, the harder question becomes which strategy to bet on. Levels 1–8 increased delegation and autonomy. Levels 9 and 10 extend how agents explore and test strategies. Level 9 pits strategies against each other to find the best one, and Level 10 runs that competition multiple times to see which strategies win consistently.
